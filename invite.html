Fortify

I've built something. Would you help me find out whether it's any good?

What it's for

Where does my money need to go?

Almost no budgeting app answers that. They're built to tell you where your money went — last month, in categories, after the fact. Useful for a post-mortem. No help at all on a Tuesday when you're deciding something.

Fortify looks the other way. It organises everything around your pay cycle rather than the calendar month, works out what's already spoken for between now and next payday, and tells you what's genuinely left.

Other apps
"You spent $380 on groceries last month." Interesting. Doesn't help me decide anything today.
Fortify
"$214 safe to spend until Thursday week — and rego is due in three." That I can act on.

The part I think matters most

You see the big ones coming.

Rego. Car insurance. Christmas. The bills that don't arrive monthly and so never make it into a monthly budget — they just turn up and hurt.

Because Fortify looks forward, those appear on the horizon while there's still time to do something. You can put a bit aside each payday into a pot for each one, and watch it fill. The bill arrives already paid for.

That's the bit that turns it from a budgeting app into something that quietly helps you save — without ever asking you to "start saving".

What you don't have to do

You don't enter your groceries.

This is the bit I'd most like to be believed about. Most budgeting apps want every coffee logged, which is why most people last about eleven days. Fortify never asks.

It wants the things you're committed to — the bills that turn up whether you think about them or not. Everything else goes in as one rough number per pay cycle, in a single box, and it subtracts it. Change the number whenever it stops feeling right.

You enter
Rent, power, insurance, subscriptions, rego, the annual lumps. Once each. Most of them never need touching again.
You don't
Groceries, petrol, coffee, nights out. One estimate a cycle covers the lot. No receipts, no categories, no logging.

If you'd rather itemise every shop, nothing stops you — put them in and it'll handle them. It just never asks you to, and it doesn't sulk when you don't.

That's why setup is half an hour and not a weekend, and why keeping it going is about a few minutes a payday — which I suspect is the difference between something you're still using in November and something you aren't.

How it handles real life

Money doesn't arrive on a tidy schedule.

So it doesn't insist that it does. Have a look at the three shapes below — each lands in the right pay cycle automatically.

What helping actually involves

An evening, then a few minutes a payday.

Setting up

20–30 minutes at a computer, putting in your income and your regular bills. It's a proper sit-down job — I'd rather say so than pretend it's five minutes.

After that

A few minutes each payday. Update your balance, tick off what's been paid, see where you stand.

For how long

Two or three paydays, and I'll check in on 1 November. Whether something like this survives past the novelty is the whole question.

Then

A few short questions at the end. Ten minutes, tops.

The one thing I really need: don't be nice about it. The most useful outcome is you telling me you stopped after a week and why. Friends testing friends' projects tend to say lovely things and then quietly stop — and that teaches me nothing at all.
